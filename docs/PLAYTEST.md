# What “10/10” still needs from people

Tests can establish accounting, timing contracts and basic interoperability. They cannot establish laughter, clear instructions or an urge to replay.

The next useful test is 5–10 fresh players, not more microgames. Let them play without coaching. Ask what the first confusing moment was. Watch for repeated controls missed because of the layout, rather than because of the actual rule.

Proposed product gates (design targets, not published scientific thresholds):

- No correctly completed answer rejected. Keep the receipt JSON for any dispute.
- First meaningful action within five seconds after starting.
- At least seven of ten players voluntarily choose another run.
- Most can explain the rules of a failed task after seeing its one-sentence receipt.
- No one needs to scroll to find an answer at the supported viewport sizes.
- A first-time touch player can complete the opening sequence without opening a software keyboard or asking what to do.

Tune task durations and wording before adding systems. When changing the generator/limits, bump the challenge ruleset; do not silently make old seed links a different test. Preserve the opening's variety and the recovery task after mistakes. Keep the narrator brief and target the mistake, not the person.

Do not optimize toward surprise by hiding deadlines, changing a rule after input, or using probabilistic grading. The player should blame their haste, never the referee.
